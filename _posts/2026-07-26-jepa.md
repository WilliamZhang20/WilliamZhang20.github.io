# How Far Can a Tiny JEPA World Model Actually Control a Robot?

*Draft — not published*

There is a familiar version of model-based robotics:

1. learn a world model,
2. imagine lots of futures,
3. choose the future that reaches the goal,
4. repeat until the benchmark is solved.

I built a small robotics project to test where that story works, where it
breaks, and what has to replace it when the world contains contact, momentum,
long horizons, and a 28-DoF hand.

The model is intentionally small: a low-dimensional, action-conditioned JEPA
world model, generally under six million parameters. No pixels, no giant
foundation-model prior, no attempt to hide weak control behind scale.

The result is less like “one architecture solves robotics” and more like a set
of increasingly sharp lessons:

- representations and controllers are different things;
- demonstrations are compatible with JEPA, but copying demo actions is not the
  only way to use them;
- primitive-action planning works when the dynamics are smooth;
- contact needs an on-manifold action generator;
- long horizons need hierarchy;
- and sometimes the right world model predicts an *event*, not the next fixed
  number of frames.

Here is the progression from the easiest tasks to the hardest.

## Level 0: FetchReach — the world-model fairy tale is real

FetchReach is the clean case. The gripper moves through free space toward a
3-D target. There is no object to grasp, no discontinuous contact mode, and no
reason for the cost landscape to go flat.

This is exactly where a compact JEPA plus model-predictive control should work,
and it does: roughly 0.95–1.00 success.

The model encodes the current observation, predicts future latent states under
candidate action sequences, and the planner picks the sequence whose predicted
future is closest to the encoded goal.

This task establishes an important baseline: the learned predictor is not
decorative. On smooth, locally controllable dynamics, planning through it is
useful.

But Reach is also dangerously flattering. It makes the full robotics problem
look like continuous optimization. The next tasks remove that illusion.

## Level 1: FetchPush — data quality beats planner cleverness

FetchPush adds a block and contact.

The first version looked like a planning failure. In reality it was a data
failure: the scripted data collector almost never produced a successful push.
The world model accurately learned the world it saw—a gripper moving near a
mostly stationary block.

No cost function can extract a pushing skill from dynamics that contain almost
no pushes.

After fixing the expert so the training set actually contained the behavior,
the control problem became tractable. The winning controller is not blind
Gaussian action search. A conditional flow model proposes coherent action
chunks from the current and desired future JEPA latents:

```text
action_chunk ~ flow(z_now, z_future)
```

The JEPA predictor then checks which proposed chunk realizes the desired latent
future.

Result: 1.00 success over the checked evaluation, with about 8 mm mean final
distance.

The key division of labor is:

- the flow prior knows what plausible pushes look like;
- JEPA knows what those pushes are likely to cause.

The world model does not have to invent a temporally precise contact sequence
from white noise.

## Level 2: FetchPickAndPlace — a world model is not a controller

PickAndPlace exposes the “needle in action space” problem.

Once the gripper has a good grasp, the learned world model predicts the lift and
transport accurately. But finding that grasp by sampling primitive actions is
extremely unlikely. Before contact, object-to-goal distance barely changes, so
the planner gets almost no useful signal for the exact multi-step motion that
closes the fingers around the block.

The replacement is a future-conditioned inverse model:

```text
inverse(z_now, z_future) -> action_chunk
```

It learns from trajectory transitions, not from a runtime `state -> expert
action` lookup. At evaluation, the target is a desirable future; the inverse
model proposes an action chunk that caused that kind of transition in the data,
and JEPA ranks local variants.

Result: 1.00 success, about 11 mm mean final distance.

This was the first strong evidence for the project’s central control recipe:

> Use JEPA to represent and predict consequences. Use an on-manifold,
> future-conditioned action model to propose how to get there.

## Level 3: FetchSlide — stop predicting frames, predict the event

FetchSlide is deceptively hard. The goal is out of reach. The robot must strike
a puck with the correct impulse, lift away, and watch it coast under friction.
There is almost no corrective authority after contact.

Receding-horizon control is structurally wrong here. “Replan every few frames”
is useful when the robot can correct. Slide is “commit once, then observe.”

The early generic attempts reflected that mismatch:

- flat CEM planning: effectively zero;
- long-horizon flow and inverse priors: around 0.0–0.1;
- the hand-written geometric expert: about 0.40.

The first answer was an event-conditioned hierarchical world model. Instead of
predicting frame 1, frame 2, …, frame 24, it predicts from:

```text
pre-impact state + compact strike macro
```

to:

```text
absorbing post-coast latent + final puck displacement
```

The controller geometrically approaches the puck, asks the event model to score
goal-relative strike macros, executes one, and never replans during the coast.
That reached 0.735.

The remaining error had a strong architectural smell: the MLP was relearning
the same ballistic response at every global table orientation.

The second event model canonicalizes the problem around the live
puck-to-goal axis. Contact offset, gripper velocity, puck velocity, and strike
direction are expressed in that goal frame. Fourier features represent the
continuous strike parameters. Gated residual blocks predict the endpoint in
canonical coordinates, while the JEPA branch still predicts the future latent.
Training samples endpoint-distance quantile bins uniformly so rare accurate
strikes are not drowned out by the much larger set of misses.

One self-supervised calibration round feeds the model’s selected strike macros
and their observed coast endpoints back into the event model. There is no
reward, critic, value function, or policy gradient.

Fresh evaluation:

- 0.848 over 1,000 episodes;
- 0.857 over a second independent 1,000 episodes;
- 0.853/2,000 aggregate.

The lesson is bigger than this one benchmark:

> A hierarchy is not only about long time horizons. It can also be about
> choosing the physically correct temporal abstraction.

For a ballistic task, “impact to rest” is a better world-model step than “the
next eight simulator frames.”

## Level 4: PointMaze — hierarchy becomes unavoidable

Maze navigation is smooth at the primitive level but long-horizon at the task
level.

A local walker can move toward a nearby coordinate. It cannot point directly at
a distant goal through a wall. Extending the primitive planning horizon makes
model error compound and invites the optimizer to hallucinate wall-crossing
trajectories.

The hierarchical solution has two levels:

- a low-level future-conditioned inverse or flow controller reaches nearby
  subgoals;
- a high-level HWM predicts abstract macro transitions and proposes feasible
  macro-actions from the demonstrated distribution.

The important word is *feasible*. A Gaussian optimizer over abstract actions
still invents shortcuts through walls. A flow prior over demonstrated
macro-actions keeps high-level planning on the route manifold.

PointMaze results:

- UMaze: 1.00;
- Medium: 1.00;
- Large: 1.00.

The older implementation used an empirical reachability graph and Dijkstra.
That worked, but it stored the answer as a graph. The current controller uses a
neural flow-macro HWM high level and a directed low-level walker. Same practical
result, more general mechanism.

## Level 5: AntMaze — the failure was falling over

AntMaze combines routing with an 8-DoF locomotion system. The high-level maze
idea still works, but the low-level controller has to maintain a gait for up to
1,000 steps.

Early diagnosis: the walker was too slow.

Actual diagnosis after adding the right instrumentation: the ant was spending
roughly 38–57% of evaluation steps upside down.

This is a useful warning for world-model research. Aggregate success can make a
failure look like planning, speed, representation, or credit assignment. A
simple physical state diagnostic changed the entire direction.

The first fix was a self-righting specialist, but it exposed an architectural
smell: a hand-written posture threshold chose between two separate controllers.
The final version folds gait and 794 clean self-righting demonstrations into one
condition-modulated residual flow. Self-righting chunks are paired with random
route goals during training, so the network learns from state when those actions
are useful. There is no recovery flag or specialist switch at runtime.

This is closer to the JEPA thesis: demonstrations expand the observed behavior
manifold, the frozen latent describes the current situation, and one predictive
controller infers the relevant mode.

Current checked results:

- UMaze: 1.00/60, with all three seed blocks at 1.00;
- Medium: 0.775/80, with blocks 0.75/0.75/0.80/0.80;
- Large: 0.533/60, with blocks 0.55/0.45/0.60.

Medium and Large are not cleanly solved. Their ceiling is now mostly reliable
locomotion over extreme path lengths, not high-level route discovery. The
reported seed controls both environment initialization and stochastic flow
sampling; these are sweeps, not selected videos.

## Level 6: Adroit — demonstrations as futures, not just labels

Adroit introduces a high-dimensional dexterous hand:

- Door;
- Hammer;
- Pen;
- Relocate.

Sparse-success exploration is not a realistic way to discover these skills
from scratch in this small project. Offline demonstrations are necessary.

That does not contradict JEPA. JEPA is not a “never use demonstrations”
philosophy. Demonstrations can be observations of how the world evolves,
examples of desirable futures, and evidence for inverse dynamics. The more
interesting distinction is whether the final controller is a direct
`state -> demo action` clone or whether it reasons through future-conditioned
transitions.

### Door and Hammer: phase structure

Door and Hammer have relatively stable temporal organization.

The demonstrations are divided into self-supervised progress phases. A
phase-conditioned inverse prior tracks demo-derived future latents and proposes
short action chunks.

Results:

- Door: 1.00/30;
- Hammer: 1.00/30.

Both replaced their old behavior-cloned controllers.

### Pen: multimodality wants a flow

In-hand pen reorientation is more multimodal. The same current state can admit
several valid finger-motion sequences.

A deterministic inverse model tends to average them. A raw-plus-latent
future-conditioned flow prior preserves the modes.

Result: 0.90/30, replacing BC.

### Relocate: possession is a latent mode

Relocate was the hardest Adroit task in the project. One controller blurred
three regimes:

1. reach and establish contact;
2. transport while preserving possession;
3. place at the target.

Generic phase models, action refinement through imagined JEPA rollouts, contact
scorers, conditional VAEs, and multiple self-imitation variants all failed to
reliably close the gap.

The winning controller uses two segment-pure inverse specialists:

- a reach specialist, emphasizing the live palm-to-ball geometry;
- a held/transport specialist, emphasizing the live ball-to-target geometry.

A firm possession predicate switches between them. Each specialist tracks a
single demonstrated future trajectory instead of jumping between unrelated
nearest neighbors every replan.

Result: 0.957/210 held-out, and 0.955/330 across the broader validation.

The old BC result was 1.00 on a development check. At this scale the residual
gap was treated as evaluation noise, and the BC checkpoint was removed.

The reusable rule was:

> Never average action manifolds that correspond to different physical modes.

That rule carries directly into the hardest environment.

## Level 7: FrankaKitchen — the hard part is composition

Kitchen asks a 9-DoF arm to complete a sequence of articulated-object tasks.
The original benchmark target was:

- microwave;
- kettle;
- light switch;
- slide cabinet.

Flat controllers were terrible at full sequences. Single-step cloning, latent MPC,
Dreamer-style dynamics, and global flow policies could often complete one or
two tasks, then drift into a state from which the remaining behavior was
off-manifold.

The successful four-task controller ports the Relocate recipe:

- one segment-pure inverse specialist per object;
- demo-locked future tracking inside each specialist;
- feature emphasis matched to the skill’s dominant servo error;
- a firm completion predicate before dropping the rest of the current action
  chunk and switching specialists.

With environment completion events, it reaches 0.813/150 full-sequence success.
A learned JEPA-plus-raw completion probe trained from aligned demonstration
replays removes that runtime oracle and reaches 0.784/125, with 3.58/4 tasks on
average.

### Removing the fixed task-order assumption

A naive greedy scheduler had failed. It picked whichever task’s demonstrations
looked nearest to the current arm pose. That ignores the most important
question: what pose will this task leave for the *next* one?

The new scheduler learns a directed handoff graph from demonstration segment
boundaries:

```text
edge(a -> b) =
    distance from demonstrated terminal arm poses of a
    to demonstrated starting arm poses of b
```

A tiny dynamic program finds the minimum-cost route through whatever task subset
the user requests.

If the four specialists are supplied in the deliberately scrambled order
`slide, light, microwave, kettle`, the graph reconstructs:

```text
microwave -> kettle -> light -> slide
```

With the learned completion probe and live demo re-locking, that reordered
request scores 0.80/100 across four fresh seed blocks
(0.88/0.76/0.80/0.76). Without re-locking, fresh seeds averaged 0.62; the
earlier 0.87 single-seed block was optimistic.

This is a more precise notion of “order-agnostic.” It does not pretend every
permutation is physically sensible. It discovers a sensible order from
demonstrated handoff geometry instead of hardcoding one.

### Expanding from four tasks to all seven

The simulator actually exposes seven articulated tasks:

- bottom burner;
- top burner;
- light switch;
- slide cabinet;
- hinge cabinet;
- microwave;
- kettle.

The labeling pipeline, completion probe, specialist trainer, environment
wrapper, metrics, and scheduler now all use a dynamic task vocabulary.
Replaying the existing partial demonstrations against all seven predicates
produced enough transition coverage to train seven specialists.

The first full-seven checks looked like 0.10/10 and 0.05/20. A fresh
three-seed audit with a task-count-scaled 490-step budget is more sobering:

- 0.05/0.00/0.00 across three 20-episode blocks, or 0.017/60;
- 4.35/3.95/4.35 tasks completed, or 4.22/7 mean;
- the learned-switch check remains 4.2/7 mean over 10, with no full completion.

The hinge cabinet is the dominant residual failure. So “all seven supported” is
true; “all seven solved” is not.

That distinction matters. Generalizing the software and high-level algorithm to
an arbitrary task set is real progress. It does not magically supply missing
handoff coverage or make every low-level specialist reliable.

## What I now believe about JEPA control

The project started with a simple question: how far can a small predictive
representation go?

The answer depends on what job we give it.

### 1. JEPA is strongest as a representation and consequence model

It can encode the state needed by a controller and predict useful futures.
That does not mean unconstrained optimization through the predictor will
discover contact-rich behavior.

### 2. The action proposal distribution is part of the intelligence

Inverse and flow models are not embarrassing add-ons. They express the feasible
action manifold. JEPA evaluates consequences *within* that manifold.

### 3. Demonstrations are not philosophically forbidden

The useful question is how they are consumed. A demonstration can define a
future, teach a transition, reveal a phase, establish a handoff edge, or provide
a recovery skill. Direct action cloning is only one option.

### 4. Hierarchy should match physics, not a generic clock

For mazes, hierarchy separates route selection from locomotion.

For Kitchen, it separates task ordering from object specialists.

For Slide, it changes the world-model step from “eight frames” to “impact until
rest.”

### 5. Negative results are architecture information

When JEPA rollout optimization repeatedly fails on contact, the answer is not
always more candidates or a bigger MLP. It can mean:

- the data lacks the event;
- the action manifold is multimodal;
- two physical modes were blurred together;
- the predictor is being queried off-distribution;
- or the temporal abstraction is wrong.

The progression across these environments is not one scaling curve. It is a
sequence of finding the right factorization.

That is probably the most LeCun-compatible conclusion of all: useful
intelligence is not a giant reflex policy, but a collection of predictive
models, proposal mechanisms, objectives, memories, and actors operating at the
right levels of abstraction.


## Addendum:

 - FetchSlide is the clearest case where failures are genuinely useful to the final successful model. Its ballistic HWM learns strike→coast outcomes from a
    trial bank containing misses as well as hits, then adds unfiltered on-policy transition data; it reaches 0.853/2000. The data is structured around
    meaningful strikes, though—not arbitrary white noise.

  - FetchReach can tolerate mixed/random transitions because the dynamics and objective are smooth, but the checked training recipe still used a scripted/random
    mixture, not a proven random-only dataset.

  - FetchPush/Pick, Adroit, Kitchen, PointMaze, and AntMaze do use unsuccessful or auxiliary trajectories for coverage/world-model learning, but their fully
    successful controllers still depend on successful demonstrations or demonstrated local skills. Random failures alone have not produced the necessary grasp,
    gait, contact, or long-horizon sequencing.

  - So the honest answer is: unsuccessful rollouts can train a fully successful model when they densely cover a composable, goal-scored transition family (Slide
    is proven; Reach is plausible/mixed). They have not been sufficient when success requires discovering a rare contact mode or long action sequence. That is
    precisely the open scientific question we’re testing with tactile Shadow Hand.
